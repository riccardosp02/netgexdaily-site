methodology

From the options chain to the chart you see

Every day the data goes through seven stages, from raw collection to the interactive dashboard, with two steps still handled by hand. No proprietary backend: storage on Google Sheets, CSV publishing, client-side reading.

pipeline — 7 stages
01

Data source

At every time interval, a script reads the SPX and NDX options chain (strikes, volumes and gamma per strike) directly from available market data, via the Gexbot API. Since September, the expected move has also been added for the SPX index.

02

Collection script

The script aggregates volumes per strike and net gamma exposure, and writes it as a new row on the current day's sheet, together with the spot price and the zero gamma value.

03

Google Sheets as storage

Every trading day has its own sheet. It's not a real database, but for a personal project it's a deliberate choice: zero infrastructure to maintain, data that's always inspectable and correctable by hand when needed.

04

CSV publishing and master index

Each day's sheet is published to the web in CSV format. A separate sheet, the master index, lists all available days with their date and CSV link: it's the only source the site needs to read to know what to show in the calendar.

05

Dashboard

The page downloads the master index, populates the calendar with the available days, then downloads and parses the CSV for the selected day to draw the three charts with Chart.js, with an automatic refresh every 60 seconds on the current day.

06

Additional outputs

Two PineScript files are automatically generated at the end of the day, containing:

  • Spot price with the levels where gamma exposure is highest and lowest, and the zero gamma line.
  • The net gamma exposure.

In addition, a daily confirmation email with a direct link to the scripts.

07

Daily manual steps

The part of the process that isn't automated yet:

  • Downloading CVD data from an orderflow platform (via a dedicated script) and entering it into the daily sheet (SPX only).
  • Publishing the daily file to the web as CSV, and adding it to the master index (both SPX and NDX).
what is net gex

Why look at net gamma exposure

Net gamma exposure (net GEX) estimates how much market makers need to buy or sell on the underlying index to stay delta-neutral as the price moves, by summing the gamma of all open options across every strike.

When net GEX is positive, market makers tend to sell into rallies and buy into dips: the market compresses, and realized volatility gets dampened. When it's negative, the opposite happens: their hedging amplifies the move, and volatility tends to expand. The point where net GEX flips from positive to negative — the "zero line" — is often the level traders watch as the divide between a calm market regime and an unstable one.

positive net GEX — compressed market negative net GEX — unstable market

What are 0DTE flow effects

0DTE options (zero days to expiration) are contracts that expire in the same session they're traded. Since Cboe completed the weekly expiration calendar on the S&P 500 in 2022, there has been a listed expiry every trading day, and same-day contracts have grown into a large share of index options volume: in Cboe's published figures, on many days it's roughly half of total SPX volume.

"0DTE flow effects" are the intraday footprint this activity leaves on the underlying. Options this close to expiry carry extreme gamma near the current price, so even small index moves force market makers to make large hedging adjustments. When dealers absorb one-sided same-day flow, their re-hedging can dampen the move (buying dips and selling rallies, if they're long gamma) or amplify it, chasing the move if they're short gamma.

These effects are strike-local and short-lived: pressure concentrates on the strikes with the heaviest same-day volume, builds through the afternoon, and disappears at the close, when the contracts expire. Cboe's research generally finds the flow balanced enough that its average net impact is modest, but on days where it tilts one way, sharp intraday accelerations and "pinning" around certain strikes are often attributed to it.

Tools used

No paid service beyond the data itself: collection and automation run on Google Apps Script, storage and publishing on Google Sheets, output and frontend on free tools or ones already included in the Google/GitHub ecosystem.

limitations and next steps

What I know is still missing

limitation

Google Sheets as storage doesn't scale beyond a few hundred days and can't handle queries or aggregations: fine as a browsable archive, not for heavy historical analysis.

next

Migrate storage to a real database (e.g. Postgres/SQLite) with a small backend, to query multiple days together instead of one at a time.

limitation

Intraday data refresh relies on client-side fetching: if the browser stays closed, there's no "to-the-second" history, only the last value saved in the sheet.

next

Add more indices beyond SPX and NDX, and an alert system for when price crosses the zero gamma line.
